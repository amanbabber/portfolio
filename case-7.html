Case study 07 · Applied AI · Part 2 of 2

The photos were sharp. The submissions still failed.

Stage 1 solved image quality — real-time AI cut tower-site rejections from 18% to under 2% and killed the $700 re-climb. But within weeks of deployment the downstream data told me something uncomfortable: approvals were up, yet back-office exceptions weren't falling at the same rate. We'd fixed the camera, not the compliance gap. The rejections had just shifted category — from "blurry photo" to "wrong equipment," "wrong credentials," "recycled evidence."

The shift
Photo checker → contract engine

I evolved the system from a visual quality checker into a contract-aware compliance engine by closing three distinct gaps: a RAG pipeline that grounds the AI in each client's actual SOPs, cross-field reasoning that catches fraud no single check could see, and a deterministic trust layer that stops spoofed evidence before the model ever runs. The design principle shifted from "is this photo good?" to "does this submission satisfy the contract?"

The signal

Approvals were up, but exceptions weren't down

Stage 1 was working exactly as designed: the AI was passing photos at a far higher rate, and re-climbs had all but vanished. But when I watched the downstream metrics, back-office reviewers were still flagging a meaningful share of submissions at final approval. The volume of rejections had dropped — the category had changed.

That told me something important. We'd solved for image quality, not submission correctness. The AI could confirm a photo was sharp and well-framed. It could not tell you whether what was in the photo actually matched what the contract required.

A sharp photo of the wrong thing still passes a sharpness check. Stage 2 had to reason about meaning, identity, and provenance — none of which Stage 1 was built to see.

The diagnosis

Three gaps in the exception queue

I dug into the exception-queue data and reviewer override logs and mapped the remaining failures into three categories. Each was a fundamentally different type of risk than Stage 1 was built to catch.

Gap 1 · Quality ≠ contractual correctness

The most common rejection was no longer "blurry photo" — it was "wrong equipment" or "non-compliant installation." A technician could submit a crystal-clear photo of a battery rack and the AI would pass it, because it looked right. But the contract specified a particular mounting bolt, or a torque-spec label that had to be visible, and what was in the photo didn't match. Worse, the requirements varied by job: a rooftop small cell and a rural macro-tower upgrade have completely different checklists and specs — and the AI was applying roughly the same logic to both.

The core insight

The AI was reasoning against its own general knowledge of "what a good installation looks like." But acceptability is defined by the contract — every client, SOW, and region had different rules, and none of that context was in the system. We were asking the model to be an expert in every client's rulebook without ever showing it one.

Gap 2 · Valid in isolation, fraudulent in combination

This came from a pattern in the override logs: reviewers rejecting submissions that had passed every individual check. A technician entered a climber name and uploaded a valid climber certification. The AI passed both — the name was real, the license was real and current. But the name on the license card belonged to a different technician entirely, likely borrowed to cover a gap in crew credentials. Our system validated each field as a standalone data point. It never asked whether Field A matched the document in Field B.

Field-level validation is necessary but insufficient. In regulated workflows, the relationships between data points matter as much as the data points themselves.

Gap 3 · Recycled evidence

This was the Stage 2 equivalent of Stage 1's good-photo/bad-photo loophole, and much harder to detect. Auditing AI-approved photos, we found EXIF data that didn't match the time or location of the site the technician was on — some images taken days or weeks before the job was even scheduled. When I pulled image hashes, I found duplicates appearing across multiple, unrelated sites. The logic was simple: if a grounding lug looks the same on Tower A and Tower B, why not upload the same photo twice? The Stage 1 AI passed it both times — it was a clear, well-framed photo. But it wasn't evidence that this lug, on this tower, was installed today.

The core insight

We could validate what was in a photo but had no concept of whether it was legitimate evidence for the job in question. Without provenance — timestamps, geolocation, digital fingerprinting — our "proof of work" was trivially spoofable.

Solution 1 → Gap 1

RAG-powered contract grounding

The fundamental problem was that the model lacked specific knowledge of what each contract actually required — it reasoned from general training data, not the client's rulebook. So we built a Retrieval-Augmented Generation pipeline that injects the relevant SOPs and engineering specs into the prompt at inference time. Rather than fine-tuning or retraining a model per client, we kept the model generic and made the context proprietary.

Why RAG, not fine-tuning

Every new client's SOPs become just another document in the retrieval layer — contract enforcement scales without scaling engineering effort. And because the SOPs are retrieved, not trained on, customer contract data stays proprietary and is never used to train a model.

Solution 2 → Gap 2

Cross-field semantic reasoning

We already had conditional form fields. The missing piece was relational validation — evaluating fields as a connected cluster rather than independent inputs. So we introduced Linked-Field Inference: when a technician enters a climber name, uploads a certification, and is assigned to a job, the system evaluates all three as a single logical unit. It cross-references the name input against the OCR-extracted text from the license image against the job-assignment metadata. If any link breaks, it flags the mismatch before submission.

Solution 3 → Gap 3

A deterministic trust layer — and it runs first

The recycled-evidence problem couldn't be solved with AI reasoning alone. Provenance is a binary question — either a photo was taken at the right place and time, or it wasn't — so it deserved hard, deterministic checks, not probabilistic model output. We built an enforcement layer that verifies provenance at the point of capture:

  • Temporal guards — reject any photo whose EXIF timestamp falls outside the job's execution window.
  • Hash-based duplicate detection — flag any photo already submitted to a previous job site.
  • GPS validation — confirm the capture location matches the assigned site coordinates.

The sequencing was the decision

The trust layer runs first. Only evidence that passes provenance is forwarded to the AI for content validation — so the model never wastes tokens reasoning about photos that are already disqualified, and spoofed evidence never reaches it. Cheaper and safer, in one ordering choice.

What it proves

From "AI checks photos" to "AI enforces contracts"

The reusable lesson

An AI feature is rarely "done" when the demo works — the failures just move somewhere quieter. The discipline is watching the downstream data, seeing which category of exception the fix revealed, and knowing when a problem needs a model (contract nuance), a relationship (cross-field), or a hard deterministic gate (provenance) — and in what order to run them.
