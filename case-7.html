Case study 07 · Applied AI · Part 2 of 2

I shipped the fix, then watched what it didn't fix.

Stage 1 worked. Photos got checked on the tower instead of in an office two days later, and the share of packages sent back for rework fell from 18% to under 2%.

I kept watching the numbers anyway, and something didn't line up. Approvals were up, but reviewers downstream were still sending submissions back — fewer than before, but not as few as the rejection rate implied. So I read what they were actually rejecting. Almost none of it said "blurry" any more. It said the part in the photo was the wrong part. The certification uploaded didn't belong to the technician on site. The photo had been taken somewhere else entirely.

What changed
From "is this readable?" to "is this right?"

The first system asked whether a photo was good enough to read. It had no idea what the photo was supposed to show. That's a different question, and it needed three separate things: a way to put each client's actual specifications in front of the model, a way to check fields against each other instead of one at a time, and some plain checks on where and when a photo was taken — which don't need AI at all.

The pattern is the same in all three. Nothing was wrong with the photograph. It was sharp, well framed, correctly exposed. What was wrong was the thing it showed, or who took it, or where.

Stage 1 couldn't see any of that, and not because the model was weak. It was because of what we handed it. We told it what the photo was of — this is the grounding lug shot, this is the battery rack — and asked whether the image was usable. We never told it what the contract required of it.

The diagnosis

Three kinds of failure

I went through the exception queue and the reviewer override logs — the record of what reviewers sent back and why — and sorted what was left. It fell into three kinds, each failing for a different reason.

1 · A clear photo of the wrong part

A technician submits a sharp, well-framed photo of a battery rack. The model passes it, because it looks like a correctly installed battery rack. But this client's contract specified a particular mounting bolt, or required the torque-spec label to be visible in shot, and neither is what's in the picture.

This got worse the more clients we had. A rooftop small cell in a city and a macro tower upgrade in a rural county are different jobs with different checklists, different specs, sometimes different regional rules. The model applied roughly the same judgement to both, because roughly the same judgement was all it had.

What we'd actually asked for

The model was reasoning from its own general sense of what a good installation looks like. But what counts as acceptable here isn't general — it's whatever this client's contract says. We were asking it to be an expert in every client's rulebook without ever showing it one.

2 · Fields that were fine on their own

This came out of the override logs: reviewers rejecting submissions that had passed every check we ran.

A technician enters a climber's name. They upload that climber's certification. Both pass — the name is a real technician, the certificate is real and current. But the name printed on the certificate is someone else's, most likely borrowed to cover a crew that was short a qualified climber that day.

We checked each field on its own. We never asked whether the name in one field matched the document in the next.

Each field is valid. The problem is the relationship between them, which is the one thing nothing in the system was looking at.

3 · Photos from somewhere else

Auditing photos the AI had already approved, we found EXIF data that didn't match where the technician was or when they were there. Some had been taken days or weeks before the job was scheduled. When I compared image hashes, the same photo was turning up across unrelated sites.

The reasoning isn't hard to follow, and it's the same instinct as the two-photo trick in Stage 1. If a grounding lug on Tower A looks much like the one on Tower B, why climb twice? Stage 1 passed it both times, because both times it was a clear, well-framed photo of a grounding lug. It just wasn't evidence that this lug, on this tower, had been installed that day.

What we could and couldn't tell

We could check what was in a photo. We had no way to tell whether it belonged to the job it was submitted for. Without checking time and location, a photograph proves that something was done somewhere, once — not that this work happened here, now.

The fix · 1

Showing the model the contract

The fix for the first problem was to stop expecting the model to know things we'd never told it.

Every client already had this written down — installation specs, method statements, the standard operating procedures their own engineers work from. It just lived in documents that nothing in the checking system had ever been connected to.

So we built a retrieval step — retrieval-augmented generation, if you want the term for it. When a photo comes in, the system looks at which job it belongs to — which client, which region, which type of installation — pulls the sections of that client's specifications that apply, and puts them in front of the model along with the photo. The model isn't recalling what a good install looks like in general any more. It's reading this client's requirements and checking the photo against them.

Why we retrieved the specs instead of training on them

The alternative was fine-tuning a model per client. We didn't, for two reasons.

Adding a client this way means adding documents — no retraining, no extra model to maintain. That matters when you're onboarding clients continuously.

And retrieval keeps their specifications out of any training process. Their security teams had already been strict about where data could go, so "your specs are read at check time and never trained on" was the version they could agree to.

The fix · 2

Checking fields against each other

We already had forms that could show or hide fields depending on the answers. What we didn't have was any check that ran across fields.

This sounds like it should have been there from the start. It wasn't, for a boring reason: forms get built one field at a time, each with its own validation rule. Nobody writes the rule that spans two fields, because no single field owns it.

So we added one. When a technician enters a climber's name, uploads that climber's certificate, and is assigned to a job, the system now treats those three as one thing to check together. It reads the name off the certificate image, compares it to the name typed into the form, and compares both against who is actually on the crew roster for that job. If they don't agree, it says so before the submission goes anywhere.

The fix · 3

Some checks don't need AI

The third problem didn't need a model at all.

Whether a photo was taken at the right place and time isn't a judgement call. The answer is already in the file. Phones record when a photo was taken and where, and an image can be fingerprinted so you can tell whether you've seen it before. Those are yes-or-no questions, and putting a model on them would only add cost and uncertainty to something you can just look up.

So we put three plain checks in front of the AI:

  • If the timestamp falls outside the window when the job was actually being done, reject it.
  • If the image is a duplicate of one already submitted — to this job or any other — flag it.
  • If the location doesn't match the site the technician was assigned to, reject it.

Why the order matters

Running the plain checks first does two things at once. Evidence that shouldn't count never reaches the model, so there's nothing for the model to be talked past. And we don't spend model time on submissions that were already disqualified — which kept the running cost down as we kept adding checks.

Outcome

I'll be straight about the evidence. Stage 1 has a clean before-and-after, because the rejection rate was measured the same way on both sides of the change. Stage 2 went in gradually, check by check, so I don't have a single number I trust the same way.

What I can say is what these checks now catch that nothing caught before: a part that doesn't match the specification, a certificate belonging to someone who wasn't on site, a photo taken three weeks earlier somewhere else. All three of those used to land on a reviewer's desk — or get approved.

What I'd take to the next one

Shipping the fix is when you start learning what was actually wrong. Stage 1 worked, and the failures it cleared away were sitting on top of a second set underneath. If I'd stopped when the rejection rate dropped, all three of these would still be running.

The other one: not everything wants a model. Of the three fixes here, one needed the model to read a specification, one needed two fields compared against each other, and one just needed the timestamp on a file. Working out which is which — and running the cheap, certain checks first — was most of the design.
