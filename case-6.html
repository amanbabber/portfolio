How we stopped a blurry photo from sending a crew back up a 200ft tower

In US telecom, the product isn't the tower — it's the digital evidence that the tower was built right: a Close-Out Package of 100+ photos captured on a 200ft site. Reviewers checked that package in the back office 48–72 hours later. By then the crew was 50 miles away on another job. One blurry serial number meant sending them back up the tower — a $700 re-climb for a 10-second photo, with the project's cash frozen until it was fixed.

Context

In telecom, evidence is cash

Scopeworker is the enterprise ERP that operators like T-Mobile and Samsung use to run field execution — tower installs, 5G upgrades, fibre maintenance. Every job produces a Close-Out Package (COP): 100+ images and videos that prove the work was done correctly. The COP verifies engineering compliance (was the equipment mounted at the right height and angle?), safety (are the grounding lugs properly secured?), and contractual completion against the Scope of Work.

The COP sits where operations meets finance, and the rule is blunt:

No approved COP = no payment

Until the package is manually reviewed and approved, the project stays "open." Suppliers can't be paid, and for a large deployment, millions in capital sit frozen on the balance sheet. A single missing photo doesn't just fail a checklist — it stalls the entire cash-flow cycle.

The problem

The feedback loop arrived days too late

The core issue was never technician effort — it was timing. In the legacy workflow, the reviewer was a post-mortem auditor, not a real-time partner. By the time anyone knew a photo had failed, fixing it was maximally expensive.

Because the feedback arrived days late, the only fix was to send the whole crew back — the $700 "truck roll."

  • Financial waste — every re-climb cost roughly $700 in fuel, labour, and opportunity cost, and raised compliance risk.
  • Safety liability — a second climb up a 200ft tower for a 10-second photo is the highest-risk, lowest-value activity in the industry.
  • Frozen capital — projects sat in "Pending" for days, holding millions in un-billable revenue hostage to a minor documentation error.
The discovery

An invisible tax, found in the line items

The most dangerous problems in an enterprise are the ones everyone accepts as "just part of the job." This one was never raised as a feature request — I found it in the spend data.

While auditing line-item spend over a year, I noticed a steady uptick in Change Orders. In telecom these are usually waved off as "unforeseen site conditions," but the volume looked systemic. So I audited the line items inside those change orders, and the same three kept recurring: Secondary Site Visit, Evidence Re-capture, Administrative Re-climb.

The forensic number

1 in every 6 contracts carried a change order tied to documentation rework, averaging $700. Scaled across the history, over 120,000 contracts had been hit by these hidden costs. This wasn't a minor leak — it was a multi-million-dollar operational tax nobody had put a name to.

To understand why it had never been flagged, I interviewed field technicians and reviewers. The problem was so embedded that no one saw it as a problem with a solution — a textbook normalization of deviance:

  • The technician: "Sometimes the wind blows or the light's bad and the photo's blurry. You just drive back and fix it later. That's tower work."
  • The reviewer: "We reject about 20% of submissions. It's always been that way. We just wait for the new evidence."
The deeper problem

The system was quietly rewarding failure

The interviews surfaced something worse than sloppiness. Because a "Secondary Site Visit" change order paid out ~$700, some field agents had engineered an easy win — and it had gone undetected for years.

A moral hazard, not just a quality issue: the delayed feedback loop had become a hidden revenue stream for subcontractors, inflating every project's burn rate.

This reframed the whole strategy. We weren't building a clarity checker — we were building an anti-fraud engine. Moving validation to real time, with live on-site capture, breaks the loop at its root: if the AI demands a clear photo now, there's no rejected photo to convert into a future payout, and metadata (GPS + timestamp) proves the "good" photo was taken live, not pulled from a gallery later.

The insight

It was a latency gap, not a skill gap

The industry had built manual workarounds for a broken feedback loop and accepted them as the cost of doing business. But the failure wasn't the blurry photo — it was that by the time the technician knew they'd failed, they were already 50 miles away at the next site. Collapse that latency from days to seconds, and the entire re-climb economy disappears. That was the opportunity: turn an invisible tax into a competitive advantage.

The solution

A real-time quality gate, in the technician's pocket

I redesigned the submission workflow around one principle: prevent rework, don't manage it. The reviewer moved from a back-office desk in another time zone directly into the technician's hands.

1 · Multimodal LLM validation

Rule-based checks fail in the messy real world of shadows, glare, and rust, so we used LLM-powered multimodal reasoning. When a technician captures a photo — say, a grounding-lug attachment — the system doesn't count pixels; it reasons against 10+ workflow-specific parameters and gives feedback before the technician unharnesses.

Illustrative — the on-tower feedback, not a real screenshot
CAPTURE · grounding lugon-site
Reasons against 10+ params: lug present? · framing wide enough to see the connection point? · serial number legible despite sun glare?
✕ serial number obscured "Tilt the camera ~10° left and retake" — while still on the tower
The check happens at the moment of capture, not 72 hours later at a desk. A failure now costs 10 seconds, not a $700 truck roll.

2 · A security-first model call

Operating in US telecom infrastructure makes data sovereignty a hard prerequisite, not a preference. I benchmarked multiple model families and made the explicit call to disqualify offshore models (e.g. DeepSeek) despite strong reasoning — they couldn't meet US security and compliance requirements. We standardized on OpenAI's multimodal models, which let our Tier-1 clients pass their own internal security audits. A technical choice that was really a business-enablement choice.

3 · Co-pilot, not digital cop

If the AI just said "Rejected," technicians would hate it — and a tool field crews resent doesn't get used. So we pivoted to guided remediation: instead of a generic error, the AI gives a specific fix ("the serial number is in shadow; tilt 10° left and retake"). By helping technicians get it right while still in position, the tool became a partner that saved them a $700 mistake, not a hurdle between them and their payout.

4 · Turning a cost into a value-add

High-reasoning LLMs are expensive, and most teams treat token cost as a margin-killer. I reframed it as a risk premium: a sub-$1.00 validation call is an insurance policy against a $700 operational loss. That 700× framing turned the decision binary for stakeholders.

The reusable lesson

The win wasn't the model — it was moving the feedback loop. The same real-time gate fixed quality, killed a fraud loop, improved safety, and accelerated cash, all by asking the question while it was still cheap to answer.
