Case study 06 · Applied AI · Part 1 of 2

The same site visit, billed twice. It took a spend audit to see it.

When a carrier like T-Mobile needs equipment installed or swapped out on a cell tower, it doesn't send its own people. It hires a contractor, who sends a technician to the site. Nobody from the carrier is there to watch, so the proof is photos and videos: the technician climbs 150 to 200 feet, does the job, and records all of it on a phone camera — equipment at the specified angle, grounding wires secured, serial numbers readable. Around a hundred images and 20 videos per site. Each one has to be approved before anyone gets paid.

I was auditing a year of line-item spend when I noticed change orders climbing. In this industry those usually get written off as "unforeseen site conditions," so nobody had looked closely. I read the line items inside them, and the same few kept repeating: secondary site visit, evidence re-capture, administrative re-climb.

What made it a leak was where they sat. Every contract's original scope already paid for a technician to travel to the site. These were change orders — a second visit, added after the fact, billed on top. Same line item, charged twice. And the second trip produced no new work: someone drove back out, climbed the tower again, and retook a photograph. Over three years it had happened 21,000 times, at an average of $337 a trip — about $7.1 million.

From inside, it didn't look like anything. A reviewer opened each photo package two or three days after the job, by which point the crew was 50 miles away on another site. If one image was blurry or framed so you couldn't see the connection point, the only remedy left was to send someone back. Technicians thought re-climbs were part of tower work. Reviewers thought a 20% rejection rate was normal. It was only visible as a pattern in the spend.

I was the PM for Scopeworker, the platform the carrier and its contractors both run this work on. The problem wasn't that technicians took bad photos — it was that they found out three days too late to do anything about it. So we moved the check to the moment of capture. The technician's phone runs each photo past an AI model before they come down, and tells them what to fix while they're still in position: "the serial number is in shadow, tilt left and retake." A check costs under a dollar. It replaces a $337 trip.

The audit turned up one more thing I hadn't gone looking for. Some crews had been submitting a bad photo deliberately, because the rejection triggered a return-visit payment they could collect without ever returning.

Context

What the package is, and why it holds up payment

In the platform, that bundle has a name — the Close-Out Package. It's a contractual deliverable in its own right, the same as the physical work. A reviewer opens it and checks three things:

  • Was the equipment installed to spec — the right height, the right angle, the right hardware?
  • Is it safe — grounding wires secured, lugs torqued, nothing left loose 180 feet up?
  • Does it match what was actually ordered, line item by line item?

Only once that clears does the contract close and the invoice go out.

That order of operations is what turns a blurry photo into a money problem. The contractor has already paid its crew, fuelled the truck, and done the work. It can't bill for any of it until the package is approved. So the wait lands on the contractor, not on the carrier.

Why one photo could hold up a payment

There was no partial credit. The package cleared or it didn't, and until it cleared, the contractor hadn't been paid for work it had already done and already covered the cost of. One unreadable serial number was enough to hold the whole thing.

The problem

By the time anyone looked, the crew was gone

Nobody in this chain was careless. The technician took the photos they were asked for, in the conditions they had — wind, glare, a phone held one-handed at 180 feet. The reviewer checked them properly. What went wrong was the distance between those two moments.

The technician had no way to know. On a phone screen in bright sun, the photo looked fine. Two days later on a desktop monitor, the serial number wasn't legible. By then the only person who could retake it was on another tower in another county.

That gap cost money in the obvious way: $337 a trip, 21,000 times over three years.

The part that bothered me more was the climb itself. Tower work is dangerous — falls, weather, hardware failure. Every one of those trips put a person back up a 150-to-200-foot structure to retake a photograph. Whatever the good reasons are for sending someone up a tower, that isn't one of them.

The discovery

How I found it

I wasn't investigating photo quality. I was doing a routine review of line-item spend, looking for anything moving in a way I couldn't explain.

Change orders stood out — they were growing faster than contract volume. Change orders are normal in this business. A site turns out to need a different mount, the ground won't take the anchor. They're almost always filed as "unforeseen site conditions," and that phrase is broad enough that nobody interrogates it.

So I stopped looking at change orders in aggregate and read what was inside them. The same few line items kept coming back:

  • Secondary site visit
  • Evidence re-capture
  • Administrative re-climb

Then I checked those against the original contracts they were attached to. That's where it stopped being a pattern and became a finding: the original scope already included a site visit. The change order was adding a second one.

What the audit came back with

21,000 of these change orders over roughly three years, averaging $337 — about $7.1 million. Every one of them paid for a trip the original contract had already covered.

That told me what was happening, not why nobody had raised it. So I talked to people at both ends.

  • The technician: "Sometimes the wind blows or the light's bad and the photo's blurry. You just drive back and fix it later. That's tower work."
  • The reviewer: "We reject about 20% of submissions. It's always been that way. We just wait for the new evidence."

Neither of them was complaining. That's what made it hard to see. It wasn't a broken process anyone was fighting — it was just how the work went. A workaround had repeated long enough that it stopped registering as a workaround.

The deeper problem

Some of it wasn't accidental

Talking to field crews turned up something I hadn't expected. Not every bad photo was bad by accident.

The incentive was sitting in plain view. A rejected photo triggered a return-visit change order worth about $337, paid to the same contractor whose photo had been rejected. So the sequence was straightforward: take two photos of the same equipment, one usable and one deliberately not. Submit the bad one. Wait for the rejection. Then submit the good photo you'd had on your phone the whole time, and bill for the trip you never made.

I want to be careful how I characterise this. I don't know how much of the 21,000 was deliberate and how much was ordinary bad luck with wind and light. The data can't separate them, and I'm not going to pretend it can.

What I could see is that the system paid the same either way. We had built a process where a failed photo was worth more than a good one. That's a design problem before it's an integrity problem.

It also raised the bar for what the fix had to do. Catching a bad photo at the moment of capture removes the rejection, and without a rejection there's no return-visit payment to collect. But that only holds if the replacement photo has to be taken there and then — so capture had to be live, with location and timestamp recorded, not something you could upload later from your gallery. The solution

Moving the check from the office to the tower

The fix was to ask the same question — is this good enough to prove the work? — at a point where the answer is still useful.

1 · What the check actually does

Simple rules couldn't do this. A rule can confirm a file exists, or that it's above some resolution. It can't tell you whether a serial number is readable through sun glare, or whether the frame is wide enough to show that the grounding wire actually meets the lug. Real sites have shadows, rust, and rain on the lens.

So we used a multimodal model. It takes each photo or video clip along with a description of what that item is meant to show, and answers in plain terms. For a grounding lug it's checking about ten things: is the lug in frame, is the connection point visible, is the bolt seated, is the serial legible, is the angle close to what the spec asks for.

The technician gets the answer before they unclip from the tower.

The check runs at the moment of capture, not two days later at a desk. Getting it wrong now costs ten seconds. Getting it wrong before cost $337 and a second climb.

2 · Why we ruled out some models

This runs on infrastructure for US carriers, so there are rules about where data can go and who can process it. We tested several model families. Some of the offshore ones — DeepSeek among them — reasoned well on our test images, and I ruled them out anyway. Our clients' security teams would never have approved data going to those providers, and a model that can't clear their review is worth nothing to us regardless of how it scores.

We went with OpenAI's multimodal models, which those security teams could sign off on. The performance gap was small. The approval gap decided it. The same constraint shaped how we handled client specs in Stage 2 →

3 · Making it useful to the person holding the phone

A model that only said "Rejected" would have been worse than nothing. The technician is 180 feet up, harnessed, holding a phone, and now knows something is wrong but not what. They'd retake it at random and hope.

So it returns what to change: "the serial number is in shadow — tilt about 10 degrees left and retake." Field crews were the group most likely to reject this tool, and the specific instruction is most of the reason they didn't.

4 · Who pays for the AI

Running a multimodal model over every photo and video in a package — around 120 items a site — isn't free, and the first question from finance was what it would add to cost per job.

The comparison made it easy. A check costs under a dollar. The trip it prevents cost $337. At the scale we'd measured, the company had spent $7.1M over three years on repeat visits that these checks would have prevented most of.

The rejection rate is the number I trust most, because it's measured the same way before and after: of the packages submitted, how many came back. It went from about 18% to under 2%.

What I can't give you is a clean savings figure. The $7.1M is what the problem had cost over three years — not what we recovered. Repeat-visit change orders fell sharply after launch, since they're a direct consequence of rejections, but I'd be guessing if I attached a dollar number to the first year. I'd rather leave it as the rate.

Two things came out of it that weren't on the original plan.

Reviewers stopped being the first line of defence. Most of their time had gone to catching the obvious — blurry, wrong angle, missing label. Those don't reach them any more, so what's left in the queue is the genuinely ambiguous: an installation that looks unusual, a spec that doesn't quite match the site. That's the work that actually needs a person.

And people stopped climbing towers to retake photographs. That one has no number attached and it's the one I care about most.

What it didn't fix

Moving the check earlier solved whether the photo was usable. It didn't touch whether the photo showed the right thing. Within weeks of launch, approvals were up but the back office was still sending submissions back — wrong equipment, wrong credentials, evidence recycled from another site. That became Stage 2 →
