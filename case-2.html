Case study 02 · AI product

Stopping duplicate work orders before clients paid twice

Across 1M line items and 300,000 sites, a buyer could unknowingly order the same job twice — same site, different wording, under a different contract — and the client would pay for it twice. There was no reliable way to catch it before billing.

As the sole PM, I led the build of a two-layer detection system: deterministic rules for the obvious matches, and AI-assisted triage for the ambiguous, free-text orders. A problem we first treated as data-matching turned out to be a product, domain, and change-management problem — and managing that is what the project became about. The result wasn't more alerts. It was turning a check buyers avoided into a focused workflow they trust and act on.

The turn

We planned for a matching problem. We found a comprehension problem.

Before this program, ScopeWorker had no duplicate detection at all — this was net-new. So we started where it seemed obvious to start.

What we believed

Catching duplicates is a comparison problem. Surface line items that share an ID at a site, show the buyer the prior matches, let them decide. We built exactly that first — and for structured, fixed-cost orders, it worked.

What broke

For variable-cost, free-text orders, "surface every match" produced a long, noisy list. The same job could be written two completely different ways; two different jobs could read almost identically. Buyers hit alert fatigue and skipped the check entirely — so the one safeguard against paying twice was quietly catching nothing.

What we learned

For free-text orders, deciding whether two are the same isn't comparison — it's reading comprehension grounded in procurement knowledge. That's what turned "a matching feature" into a model, a domain rubric authored with the client, and a labeling program. The real scope of the project was managing that uncertainty, not tuning an algorithm.

What was at risk

A check that looks like a control on paper but catches nothing is worse than no check — it gives everyone false confidence while the client keeps paying for duplicated work. Adoption, not model accuracy, was the thing that could kill this.

The system

Two layers — cheap rules first, AI only where it's needed

The core bet was to spend AI only where nothing else would work. Everything a rule could catch, a rule caught. The expensive, ambiguous cases — and only those — went to the model.

The decisions

Three calls that shaped the product

01 Rules before AI
Evidence
Fixed-cost orders carry standardized specs — their duplicates are detectable with plain, deterministic logic.
Options
Send everything to the model (simple to build, expensive and opaque), or split the problem by tractability.
My decision
Solve every case a rule can solve with a rule. Reserve AI for the genuinely irreducible free-text case.
Trade-off
Two systems to maintain instead of one, and a harder story to tell than "we used AI."
Consequence
Cheap, reliable coverage shipped fast — and it de-risked the funding case for the hard layer by proving the model on the easy one first.
02 A throwaway harness before any integration
Evidence
We didn't yet know if the model could even do the job. Wiring an unproven model into the platform is the most expensive way to find out.
Options
Build inside the product and iterate live, or build a standalone checker decoupled from the platform.
My decision
Build a standalone experimentation harness first — cheap to iterate on prompts and approach, running in parallel with Layer 1.
Trade-off
Up-front time on scaffolding that ships to no user, and the discipline to not "just integrate it."
Consequence
By the time we integrated, the hard questions were answered. Wiring the AI layer in took only ~3–4 weeks because the research was already done.
03 0.78 — fewer false alarms, and accept more misses
Evidence
The old noisy check already proved that a queue people don't trust gets abandoned — and then catches zero.
Options
A low threshold (catch more, flood the queue) or a high one (cleaner queue, more misses).
My decision
Score the model against the client-labeled golden set across cutoffs and ship 0.78 — just right of the crossover.
Trade-off
We knowingly let some real duplicates slip rather than risk drowning the signal.
Consequence
A queue buyers actually work. A slightly leakier filter people trust catches far more over time than a strict one they ignore.

That last call is the whole feature in one picture. Here's how to read it:

How to read this chart
  • The horizontal axis is the confidence threshold — how sure the model must be before it flags a pair. Move left and it flags more; move right and it flags fewer.
  • The red line — false positives — is wrong flags. Loosen the threshold (left) and these pile up, burying real matches in noise and bringing back the fatigue that killed the old check.
  • The teal line — false negatives — is real duplicates the model misses. Tighten the threshold (right) and more of these slip through to billing.

The two lines always move in opposite directions: every cutoff buys fewer of one error by accepting more of the other. No setting makes both zero — so the job isn't to eliminate error, it's to pick the point where the trade is worth it.

A slightly leakier filter people trust and actually triage catches far more cumulative leakage over time than a stricter one they ignore.
The iteration

How the AI layer actually got good

The harness earned its keep here — it's where three problems surfaced and got solved before any of this touched a real buyer.

1 · One giant prompt wasn't enough

We started with a single macro prompt — one global "are these two line items duplicates?" applied to everything. It wasn't good enough. The fix was architectural, not a threshold tweak: per-item-type prompts, so the model is told how to judge duplicates for the specific kind of work in front of it.

✕  before · one macro prompt
"Are these two line items duplicates?" → applied to cranes, antennas, fiber, everything.
✓  after · per item-type
crane_rental: weigh capacity, duration... antenna_install: weigh sector count, band... fiber_run: normalize metres ↔ feet...

2 · The rubric — defining "correct" with the people who own the domain

An LLM will invent its own fluid definition of a "duplicate" if you let it. I authored a scoring rubric that pins the model to real procurement logic — but the ground truth couldn't come from me. T-Mobile's procurement team owned the labeling of the golden set; I built the rubric with them and ran the sessions.

SignalWeightRule
PurposePrimaryAny difference means distinct jobs. A foundation pour ≠ an antenna install.
SitePrimaryDifferent site → not a duplicate. Site code and site name are normalized as the same signal.
Time windowPrimarySame work at the same site months apart is legitimate re-work, not a duplicate order.
Scope detailsSecondaryAn operator present on one crane rental and absent on the other is a different scope of work.
PriceWeakHeld to a tolerance, never decisive alone — prices legitimately move with quotes and timing.
Change management, not just product

Most of these domain experts had never been asked to be the judge in a labeling exercise. A lot of the work was getting them there — why label quality was non-negotiable, how we'd chunk it to be doable, what we were building. Their disagreements on hard pairs weren't friction; they were signal, exposing exactly where the rubric was underspecified, which fed straight back into tightening it.

3 · The client stayed in the loop the whole way

We didn't rely on a single after-the-fact metric. The harness was the measurement loop: real line-item pairs ran through it continuously, and T-Mobile's procurement team watched the outputs throughout. The people who defined "correct" were the same people confirming the system met their bar — in the loop the whole way, not just at a launch sign-off.

Outcome

A check buyers avoided became one they act on

The program took ScopeWorker from no duplicate detection to a layered system: deterministic coverage for the structured cases, and an AI triage layer that collapses a site's messy variable-cost history into a short queue a buyer can clear in seconds.

The real win wasn't time saved — it was behavior changed. The safeguard against paying twice went from a noisy list everyone ignored to a workflow buyers trust and actually use, so duplicates get caught at the point of procurement, before they ever reach billing. And because the client's own experts defined and validated "correct," it shipped with their trust built in, not bolted on.

The reusable lesson

The model was never the moat. Adoption was the constraint — so the work was sequencing AI to only the irreducible case, defining "correct" with the domain experts who own it, and owning the trade-off between crying wolf and letting a miss slip through.

What I'd do differently

I'd instrument the queue as a first-class product surface from day one — flagged-vs-confirmed rate, triage time, and duplicates prevented, captured continuously. We validated adoption by watching real behavior with the client, which is strong qualitative evidence; but the version of this story with a hard before/after number on leakage caught is the one I'd rather be able to tell, and that starts with measurement built in from the first sprint.
