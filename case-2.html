TL;DR

Sequencing was the strategy

Sole PM on a program that took ScopeWorker from no duplicate detection to a two-layer system catching double-ordered work before it reaches billing — across 1M line items and 300,000 sites. I sequenced it deliberately: built the deterministic layer first (ID-detection foundation, then standardized fixed-cost items), and reserved AI for the irreducible variable-cost case.

For that case I authored the scoring rubric, ran golden-set labeling with T-Mobile's procurement team as the domain owners, de-risked with a standalone experimentation harness before integrating, and set the 0.78 confidence threshold by optimizing for queue trust over raw recall.

The bet

Use AI only where it's genuinely irreducible. Define "correct" with the people who own the domain. Sequencing was the strategy — not the model.

The problem

Zero detection, at enterprise scale

ScopeWorker manages infrastructure procurement at scale: a single enterprise program spans roughly 1M line items across 300,000 sites. Procurement is site-specific, and buyers order complex work as line items — a "40ft crane for a foundation pour," an antenna-install crew. The same job could be ordered twice at the same site under different contracts, unnoticed, and the client would pay for it twice.

Before this program, there was no detection of any kind. The capability didn't exist, so this wasn't a feature enhancement — it was net-new infrastructure, built from zero.

I owned it end to end as sole PM: what got built and in what order, who worked on it, the timelines, the client relationship, and how we knew it was working. The team was deliberately small — one senior full-stack engineer, one junior front-end engineer, and one QA — so sequencing and scope discipline mattered more than headcount.

Program design

Two layers, escalating in cost and cleverness

The core product bet: don't reach for AI first. Solve everything you can with deterministic logic, and reserve AI for the one case that genuinely can't be solved any other way. The result is a two-layer detection system — a cheap, reliable deterministic layer underneath, and a targeted AI layer on top of only the hard case.

Layer What it covers How it works Why this order
Layer 1 Deterministic ~6 wks foundation, then fixed-cost ID-matched items, plus fixed-cost items with standardized specs. Structured, rule-based comparison. No AI — using it here would be over-engineering. The tractable case. Built first: the ID-detection foundation, then fixed-cost. Delivered coverage fast and proved the model before funding the hard case.
Layer 2 AI triage 3–4 wks to integrate Variable-cost, free-text items — collapsed into a short, high-signal queue. Semantic similarity with an LLM on top, per item-type prompts. Irreducible — a reading-comprehension problem, not a comparison problem. De-risked by a standalone harness before integration.
Layer 2 — why AI at all

The variable-cost case is a reading problem

Variable-cost line items are the hard case, and the reason the whole program needed AI at all.

VC items are free text with no standard specification. Two items can describe the same purchase in completely different words — a site code on one, a site name on the other; feet on one, meters on the other. And two can look nearly identical yet be different jobs, separated by a single word buried mid-string: a foundation pour is not an antenna install. Deterministic logic can't touch this.

At our scale, the first instinct — surface every ID match and let the buyer sort it out — had already failed for VC items: the list was long and mostly noise, buyers hit instant alert fatigue, and they skipped the check entirely.

The failure mode

An ignored duplicate check is worse than no check at all — it looks like a compliance control on paper while catching absolutely nothing in reality.

So the AI layer wasn't about detection volume. It was about surfacing a short, high-signal set a human would actually work — and doing it in a way the client could trust.

De-risking

A standalone harness before a single integration cycle

Rather than commit expensive integration cycles up front, I had us build a standalone, in-house AI duplicate-checker, decoupled from the platform, as an experimentation harness. It let us iterate on approach and prompts cheaply, and validate that the AI could actually do the job before we wired it into production.

This is where the real research lived — semantic-similarity approaches, and using an LLM on top of them to keep token cost down — running in parallel with the deterministic layer's build. By the time we integrated, the hard questions were already answered. Integration of the VC AI layer took roughly 3–4 weeks of development precisely because the research and POC work had de-risked it in advance.

Iteration that mattered

From one macro prompt to per-item-type prompts

The harness surfaced a real design problem. We started with a single macro prompt — one global "are these two line items duplicates?" instruction applied to everything. The results weren't good enough.

The fix was an architecture change, not a threshold tweak. I moved us to configurable line-item-level prompts. When a line item is added, the duplicate-detection logic for that item type can be configured specifically — so the model is told how to judge duplicates for the kind of work in front of it, not asked one blunt question about everything. That shift is what materially moved quality.

The rubric

Defining "correct" with the people who own the domain

An LLM will invent its own fluid definition of a "duplicate" if you let it. Getting to a consistent, trustworthy definition was the hardest — and least technical — part of the whole program.

I authored the scoring rubric that aligns the model with real procurement logic:

Signal Weight Rule
Purpose Primary Any difference means distinct jobs. A foundation pour ≠ an antenna install.
Site Primary Different site → not a duplicate. Site code and site name are normalized as the same signal.
Time window Primary Same work at the same site months apart is legitimate re-work, not a duplicate order.
Scope details Secondary An operator present on one crane rental and absent on the other is a different scope of work.
Price Weak Held to a tolerance, never decisive alone — prices legitimately move with quotes and timing.

But the ground truth couldn't come from me. The people who know whether two procurement items are truly the same are T-Mobile's procurement team — so they owned the labeling of the golden set, and I built the rubric with them and ran the sessions.

Change management, not just product

Most of these domain experts had never been asked to act as the judge in a labeling exercise. A lot of the work was getting them there: explaining why the effort mattered, why label quality was non-negotiable, how we'd chunk it so it was actually doable, and what we were ultimately trying to build. Their disagreements on hard pairs weren't friction — they were signal, exposing exactly where the rubric was still underspecified, which fed straight back into tightening it.

The threshold

Optimizing for queue trust, not raw recall

A raw score doesn't ship a feature; you have to choose a cutoff, and that's a product decision, not a technical one — a direct trade between two failures. Too low, and false positives flood the queue, alert fatigue returns, and buyers abandon it again. Too high, and false negatives slip silently through to billing.

The chart below is the whole decision in one picture. Here's how to read it:

How to read this chart
  • The horizontal axis is the confidence threshold — how sure the model has to be before it flags two items as a possible duplicate. Move left and it flags more pairs; move right and it flags fewer.
  • The red line — false positives — is wrong flags: two different jobs called duplicates. Loosen the threshold (left) and these pile up, burying real matches in noise and bringing back the alert fatigue that killed the old check.
  • The teal line — false negatives — is real duplicates the model misses. Tighten the threshold (right) and more of these slip silently through to billing.

The two lines always move in opposite directions: every cutoff buys fewer of one error by accepting more of the other. No setting makes both zero — so the job isn't to eliminate error, it's to pick the point where the trade is worth it.

Illustrative — shape of the trade-off, not exact eval values
False positives — noise in the queue, drives fatigue

Shipped threshold — 0.78
I scored the model against the client-labeled golden set across candidate cutoffs and landed on 0.78 — deliberately just right of the crossover. A lower bar would have caught more theoretical duplicates but flooded the queue and destroyed adoption. A slightly leakier filter people trust and actually triage catches far more cumulative leakage over time than a stricter one they ignore.
The measurement loop

How we knew it was working

We didn't rely on a single after-the-fact metric. The experimentation harness was the measurement loop: we ran real line-item pairs through it continuously, iterated the prompt architecture against what we saw, and — critically — had T-Mobile's procurement team watching the outputs throughout. The client experts who defined "correct" were the same people confirming the system met their bar, in the loop the whole way, not just at a launch sign-off.

Outcome

Behavior changed — not just time saved

The program took ScopeWorker from no duplicate detection at all to a layered system: deterministic coverage for fixed-cost items, and an AI triage layer that collapses a site's variable-cost order history into a short, high-signal queue a human can triage in seconds.

The real win wasn't time saved — it was behavior changed. A check buyers had been skipping became one they work, so duplicate purchases get caught at the point of procurement, before they ever reach billing.

The reusable lesson

The model was never the moat. The value was in sequencing the program so AI was used only where it was irreducible, defining what "correct" means with the domain experts who own it, and owning the trade-off between crying wolf and letting a miss slip through.
